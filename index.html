Through optimized training and inference, our stochastic renderer achieves significantly better image quality than prior art with the same number of random samples, making single-sample rendering viable for applications that demand high quality and extremely high frame rates.

Color Variance Regularization

Stochastic rendering draws each Gaussian along a view ray with probability equal to its blending weight, so the noise of a single sample is the variance of the colors along that ray. Standard 3DGS training, however, matches the ground truth by blending Gaussians of disparate colors. We therefore add a color variance term Lvar to the training loss, which encourages all Gaussians that splat onto the same pixel to share the same color, without any change to the renderer.

History-Based Spatial Resampling

Neighboring pixels see similar color distributions, so each sample can also serve its neighbors. Plain averaging blurs the image; instead, we weight every reused sample by how likely it is at the target pixel relative to its source pixel, which keeps the introduced bias minimal. The occlusion part of these weights would normally require sorting. Since a model trained with Lvar has very few relevant occluders per pixel, we approximate it from a small buffer of the K most recent samples at each pixel.

Why Spatial Resampling Works

Two neighboring pixels A and B usually see similar, but not identical color distributions (\(p_A \approx p_B\)). With one sample per pixel, both are noisy. With resampling, each pixel also reuses its neighbor's sample, weighted by how likely that sample is at the pixel compared to the neighbor (\(w_{B \rightarrow A} = p_A(s_B) / p_B(s_B)\)). Each pixel thus averages two samples instead of one, while samples that are unlikely at the pixel, e.g. from across an edge, get low weights and do not blur it.

Convergence

Accumulating samples over frames, resampling improves the image right away: with a single sample per pixel, it gains 4.8 dB PSNR over plain averaging. A uniform cross-shaped blur removes noise similarly at first, but plateaus at around 29.5 dB, as it blends across depth edges, while our depth- and transmittance-aware weights keep edges sharp. A Match Made in Heaven

Variance regularization and resampling reinforce each other: with Lvar, fewer Gaussians contribute to each pixel, so the occluders stored in the K-buffer match those of the neighboring pixels more closely and the resampling weights become more accurate. On these views, the combination improves the PSNR of a single sample per pixel by 7.2 to 9.7 dB over plain stochastic rendering. Drag the center handle to compare the four variants.

Spatio-Temporal Resampling

Color distributions are also coherent over time. Under mild assumptions, full spatio-temporal resampling reduces to a recursive exponential moving average along the motion trajectory. Instead of clamping the history color or resetting it at depth changes, we lower the weight of outlier history, which avoids both ghosting and popping.

Spatio-Temporal Blue Noise

Our renderer keeps a Gaussian at a pixel if a random threshold, drawn anew each frame, lies below its opacity, and the closest kept Gaussian colors the pixel. With white noise thresholds, the kept samples form clumps and holes, while spatio-temporal blue noise spreads them evenly over neighboring pixels and over frames. A single frame has the same error either way, but the evenly spread noise looks much smoother, and averaging over frames, as our temporal reuse does, removes it much faster. Sharing one blue noise texture between all Gaussians would correlate overlapping ones: the teal Gaussian is then only kept where the orange one in front of it is kept as well, so it never shows through. We therefore offset the texture per primitive.
